   Similarly to most other flaviviruses, there is a single glycan attached to a loop in domain I of the Zika virus E protein that has been highlighted as a noticeable feature and hypothesized to govern cellular tropism or contribute to disease outcomes (63).    How --eset, priors, and how alignments behave -according to Eric! 

   The effective sequence number is the parameter that controls the relative weight of the counts from the input sequence alignment 
   and the 'priors' which are kind of like generic parameters that are averaged over many different large structural RNA alignments. 
   Using 'priors' significantly improves performance for models that are built from a small number of sequences because the input alignment 
   doesn't provide a very rich representation of how structural alignments behave, just because it is so small. For example, 
   the prior provides information on how watson crick basepairs (A:U and G:C) commonly substitute for each other in structural RNA alignments.
   
   The effective sequence number parameter controls what the relative entropy of the model will be.
   The --eset parameter defines the effective sequence number, so what we are trying to do is figure out what the effective sequence number should 
   be to get a relative entropy of between 0.540 and 0.541. Once we do that, the three models will all be balanced the 
   same way, such that when they score a sequence that is say 99% similar to the model they will all give just about the same score, eg 10000 bits. 
   If the relative entropies are different than a sequence of 99% similarity will get a score of 10000 bits with the models with effective sequence 
   number of 0.540 and something like 10500 bits with the 0.591 model. This is why your last test had MG807646 classifying all the NC sequences.

   Sequences that were actually more similar to the NC model were still getting a slightly higher score with the MG model, just because it had a higher 
   relative entropy of 0.591.
